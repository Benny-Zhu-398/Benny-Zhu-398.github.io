Experience-Guided Planning for Contact-Rich Manipulation

Research · CMU Search-Based Planning Lab · Advisor: Prof. Maxim Likhachev

A MuJoCo-native planner that searches directly over physics rollouts to push a block to a goal pose, and reuses a library of previously solved pushes to cut search cost on related problems.

The problem

Sampling- and search-based planners that treat a contact-rich push as a kinematic search have to resolve every contact interaction from scratch, even when a very similar push has already been solved before. The goal is a planner that can recognize and reuse a past solution instead of re-deriving it.

Approach

Every search node stores a full MuJoCo snapshot (mj_getState/mj_setState on mjSTATE_INTEGRATION, including qacc_warmstart), so restoring a branch recomputes the exact same contacts as an uninterrupted rollout — verified to ~1e-12 by a dedicated restore-invariance test. A weighted A* (f = g + w·h) expands eight end-effector-delta primitives per node, scored by remaining pose error plus end-effector reach; success is silhouette coverage of the goal shape, not a pose threshold. Experience A* additionally lets any expanded node attach a stored push from a library of past solves, warped onto the current object pose by an SE(2) transform and screened analytically before it is ever simulated. An RRT baseline (goal-biased sampling, nearest-node expansion) is implemented for comparison. Everything runs on both a 2D puck and a 3D KUKA iiwa14 PushT task, evaluated with reproducible seeded sweeps.

My contribution

Designed and implemented the capture/restore snapshot architecture and the weighted A* / experience-A* planners built on it, the SE(2) experience-warping and lazy-attachment screening, and the ablation studies comparing plain A*, RRT, and experience A* under matched search budgets.

Results and what it is worth

On a 20-seed sweep at matched search weight, attaching a 100-push library cut mean expansions from 603 to 547 (about 9%) at the same 90% success rate; isolating a single favorable seed, reuse took it from 1,311 expansions (84 s) down to 180 (13 s). The harder finding was about execution, not search: naively replaying a stored push's joint trajectory after repositioning the arm succeeded on only 2 of 100 held-out seeds, because sub-millimeter contact offsets compound within a few pushes. Restoring the arm into the object's own contact frame — matching end-effector pose, contact points/normals, and posture, rather than raw joint angles — raised execution success to 9 of 10 on a follow-up study, at fewer expansions and less planning time than plain A*. The reuse benefit is real, but it is gated by how faithfully the stored contact state is restored, not just by having the trajectory on hand.

Dual-Arm Industrial Assembly for the RoCo Challenge

Team entry · Industrial Task Board Assembly track, 2nd RoCo Challenge @ IROS 2026 · Jan 2026 – present

A bimanual manipulation pipeline that picks nine industrial parts — gears, connectors, a pin, a rod, batteries — and assembles them onto a task board in Isaac Sim, scored part-by-part against the official evaluation harness.

The problem

The task board mixes two different placement problems: tight-tolerance “snap” connections (USB/HDMI connectors, a pin, a rod) where millimeter-scale placement error matters, and looser “drop” placements (gears onto a rack post, batteries into a cradle) that should settle into a repeatable pose under gravity — all driven from a single-arm pick-and-place harness that scores each part pass/fail independently.

Approach

The harness (Isaac Sim 5.1, a bimanual gripper with head and dual wrist cameras) hands each part to a policy implementing a shared API; snap parts are confirmed by a proximity-triggered joint once the gripper lands the part within tolerance, drop parts are graded by their settled pose. A scripted waypoint-following baseline establishes a reference. Learned policies run as a sidecar process, since Isaac Sim can't share its pinned Python environment with training stacks — adapters exist for LeRobot Diffusion Policy and pi0.5, plus a project-specific vision-conditioned diffusion policy trained per part group. Demonstrations are collected through a LeRobot-format pipeline with injected pose disturbances during collection, so the trained policy sees off-nominal starts rather than only clean scripted rollouts.

My contribution

Trained and iterated the vision-based diffusion policy for the battery-placement part group (battery_size1/battery_size5), tuning it against the disturbance-augmented dataset.

Flow Matching for Locomotion

24-782 · Team Stitch, with Gopalakrishnan Thirunellai Venkitachalam · Fall 2025

A two-stage pipeline — a symmetry-regularized PPO expert feeding a Transformer-based Flow Matching model — that produces stable, velocity-conditioned bipedal locomotion and generalizes continuously across walking speeds the expert was never trained at.

The problem

Standard reward-driven RL policies for legged locomotion default to a unimodal Gaussian action distribution, which struggles to represent the multimodal structure of natural gaits and tends to collapse into asymmetric, lopsided walking — especially at higher target speeds. Separately, an RL expert trained at a handful of discrete target velocities has no way to interpolate smoothly to a velocity it was never trained on.

Approach

Stage one trains a symmetry-aware PPO expert on the MuJoCo Walker2d biped. Starting from the default reward, we add a Gaussian velocity-tracking term and a freezing penalty (to stop the agent from dragging one leg), then compare three ways of enforcing bilateral symmetry: a plain reward-shaped policy, an auxiliary “Mirrored-PPO” loss that penalizes the policy's output disagreeing with its own mirrored-state output, and a phase-based mirroring scheme that tracks a gait-cycle phase variable and literally swaps left/right observations and actions once the agent crosses the half-cycle — training one policy to implicitly serve both legs. The expert is curriculum-trained from 0.5 m/s up to 3.5 m/s. Stage two collects a tiered, quality-filtered dataset from expert rollouts (episodes under 900 steps or near-fall frames are dropped; each trajectory is labeled by its measured average velocity rather than its commanded target, since the two diverge at higher speeds), and trains a Transformer to predict 16-step action chunks via optimal-transport Flow Matching — a deterministic alternative to diffusion that learns a velocity field along (nearly) straight-line paths between noise and data, which lets the ODE solver take much larger integration steps at inference. Online, predictions from overlapping chunks are combined with exponentially-weighted temporal ensembling to suppress jitter.

Results and what it is worth

Phase-based mirroring is the clear winner for symmetry: it cuts the Overall Symmetric Index by 83% versus a plain reward-shaped PPO (96.3 → 16.0) and by 68% versus the Mirrored-PPO auxiliary-loss baseline (49.6 → 16.0), becoming the expert used for all downstream data collection. The resulting Flow Matching policy then generalizes continuously from 0.5 to 3.0 m/s in 0.1 m/s steps — a range the discrete expert was never evaluated at outside its six training speeds — holding success rates above 80% at most velocities (100% at several) and tracking error typically under 0.4 m/s, with one weak region around 1.6–1.8 m/s where success drops to 20% and tracking error spikes, likely a mode-averaging artifact where the dataset's medium- and high-speed gait strategies overlap and conflict. Its gait symmetry (SI 18–45) lands between the phase-mirrored expert and the Mirrored-PPO baseline — looser than the expert it was trained on, but still far better than plain PPO. The practical payoff is largely computational: because the optimal-transport flow traces nearly straight paths, the ODE solver needs far fewer function evaluations than a diffusion-based alternative would, which is what makes this fast enough for closed-loop control.

Dexterous In-Hand Manipulation with Shadow Hand

With Xinxuan Tang · Mechanical Engineering, CMU

A systematic comparison of RL paradigms — PPO, SAC, and CEM — on Shadow Hand cube reorientation, plus two modifications (domain randomization, and a diffusion-based on-policy generative policy) aimed at robustness and expressiveness.

The problem

Cube reorientation with a 24-DoF Shadow Hand (157-dim observation, 20-dim action) is a high-dimensional, contact-rich control problem. A standard Gaussian policy is also structurally mismatched to it: there are multiple valid finger configurations that achieve the same target orientation, and a unimodal distribution collapses them into one averaged — often brittle — behavior.

Approach

On the Isaac-Repose-Cube-Shadow-Direct-v0 environment (dense reward: distance penalty, a rotation term that grows sharply near the goal, action regularization, a sparse success bonus, a fall penalty), we benchmark PPO (32 and 512 parallel environments, to isolate the effect of rollout parallelism), SAC, CEM, and a random baseline on identical settings. On top of that, we evaluate two modifications: (1) domain randomization over object mass, surface friction, joint damping, and observation/action noise at every reset, to fight simulator overfitting; and (2) GenPO, which replaces PPO's Gaussian policy head with an invertible diffusion policy — a doubled dummy-action construction makes the diffusion process invertible, which makes exact log-likelihoods tractable via a change-of-variables formula, so PPO's clipped surrogate objective, entropy regularization, and KL-adaptive learning rate all still apply to a genuinely multimodal policy.

My contribution

[Paper doesn't break the two-person split out explicitly — note which half was yours: the baseline sweep / domain randomization side, or the GenPO diffusion-policy side.]

Results and what it is worth

Against a random-baseline mean return of −135.13, PPO dominates SAC and CEM outright, and parallelism matters enormously: PPO with 512 environments reaches both the highest final reward and by far the highest consecutive-success score, while PPO32 plateaus much lower and SAC/CEM stay near zero for most of training — SAC's replay buffer gets flooded with low-quality early-policy transitions faster than its critic can correct for them, and CEM's sampling-based search doesn't scale to a 20-dim action space without a learned value function. Domain randomization learns slower early on but keeps improving over a much longer horizon, ending at a high, robust consecutive-success score — the expected robustness/sample-efficiency trade-off. GenPO matches or exceeds vanilla PPO's final reward with noticeably smoother, lower-variance learning curves, evidence that the multimodal diffusion policy is genuinely representing more than one viable manipulation strategy rather than collapsing to one.

Optimal Control of Quadrotor for Obstacle Avoidance

24-785 · Team 8, with Burhan Shirose, Qihang Shan, Madeline Hoedemaker

A nonconvex trajectory-optimization pipeline that steers three planar quadrotors past two moving obstacles while tracking a reference trajectory, solved and cross-checked with two different NLP solvers.

The problem

Generate dynamically-feasible, collision-free trajectories for three quadrotors navigating two moving obstacles over a 5-second horizon, minimizing trajectory-tracking error and control effort subject to the quadrotor's own dynamics, thrust limits, a minimum inter-robot separation, and a minimum clearance from each obstacle.

Approach

Each quadrotor is modeled as a 6-state planar vehicle (position, pitch, their velocities) with 2 thrust inputs; Hermite–Simpson collocation enforces dynamic feasibility between time steps. The resulting nonconvex NLP is solved two ways — Julia's fmincon (interior-point) and SciPy's SLSQP (quasi-Newton SQP) — each from multiple random initial guesses, since the collision-avoidance constraints make the feasible region non-convex and both solvers only find local minima. A sensitivity study then sweeps the cost weights (state-tracking Q, control-effort R, terminal Qf) and the safety margins (inter-robot distance, obstacle radius) against the baseline, and a Lagrange-multiplier analysis identifies exactly which constraints are actually binding the solution.

My contribution

Developed the fmincon pipeline in Julia; wrote the Problem Definition and fmincon slides, and the Project Statement and Optimization Study (fmincon part) of the final report.

Results and what it is worth

fmincon's best-of-5 multi-start run reached an objective of 326 in 83.4 s with 1.165 m average tracking error; SLSQP's best-of-10 run reached ≈328 in 585 s — seven times slower — with 1.871 m average error, so for this problem size the interior-point method was both faster and more accurate. The sensitivity sweep showed raising the state-tracking weight Q tenfold cut tracking error by 7.2% (the single most effective lever), while raising the terminal weight Qf cut final-position error by about 60% at the cost of 3.1% worse average tracking — a clean path-accuracy-vs-endpoint-accuracy trade-off. On the safety side, widening the obstacle clearance from 0.3 m to 0.8 m was the most expensive constraint, costing 8.0% more tracking error, while relaxing inter-quad spacing from 0.8 m to 0.3 m improved tracking by 4.3%; the Lagrange-multiplier analysis confirmed this directly, with the obstacle-avoidance constraints carrying the largest active multipliers (up to ≈24) at exactly the moments each quadrotor passed closest to a moving obstacle.

Tilted: A Low-Cost Mechatronic Maze-Solving Platform

24-668 Mechatronics Applications in Mechanical Engineering · with Avery Kress, Nathan O’Neill, Sander Schulman · Fall 2025

A 2-DOF tilting-plate robot that autonomously solves arbitrary laser-cut mazes: it tilts the plate to roll a ball to the goal, using an overhead camera for vision, A* for path planning, and a discrete-time LQR for control.

The problem

Guide a ball from a start cell to a goal cell through an arbitrary 2D maze cut into a square plate, actuated in roll and pitch by two hobby servos, using only a single overhead camera for sensing and an onboard battery for power — while staying within servo angle limits and without the ball leaving the maze.

Approach

Mechanically, a nested-ring gimbal (laser-cut plywood inner rings, an acrylic outer ring) carries an 8″×8″ maze plate with a whiteboard sticker so mazes can be redrawn; HS-645MG servos drive each axis through short linkages, with hard stops to about ±6°. A Raspberry Pi 5 and camera run an HSV-based pipeline to find the ball and goal and a Canny-edge pipeline to extract the maze walls into a binary occupancy map; a separate boundary-contour check keeps the ball from rolling off the plate edge regardless of the maze itself. Path planning builds a distance-transform cost map from that occupancy map and runs A*, then a low-pass filter removes the grid search's zig-zag, and a curvature-aware look-ahead selector densifies waypoints on tight turns and backs off the look-ahead distance near corners. Control linearizes the ball-on-plate dynamics (ẍ = (5g/7)θ), discretizes at 100 Hz, and runs a per-axis discrete-time infinite-horizon LQR with a feedforward term — tunable live through a GUI that visualizes closed-loop eigenvalues and step response, which doubles as a teaching tool for state-space control. An Arduino (chosen over the Pi for deterministic PWM timing) drives the servos at 0.25° resolution and runs an IMU-based auto-leveling routine before every run to compensate for mechanical drift.

Results and what it is worth

Across four canonical maze types (10 trials each), the system reached 100% success on straight-line and single-turn mazes with low steady-state error, 70% on U-turns, and 60% on multi-turn/spiral mazes. Failures concentrated on sustained-curvature paths, where accumulated friction, vision latency, and tilt saturation compound as the ball continuously changes direction — a clear limit of the linearized model rather than of the mechanism itself.
